## Part 1: The 85% Claim—What The Data Actually Shows

### The Source

The 85% figure comes from [National Police Chiefs' Council (NPCC) data from 2024](https://5pillarsuk.com/2025/01/12/police-say-85-of-group-based-paedophiles-are-white/):
- Q1-Q3 2024: 85% of group-based child abusers identified were white
- 3.9% were of Pakistani origin
- **Critical limitation**: Ethnicity was only recorded in 39% of cases

### Conflicting Data From Same Category

However, the [Home Office Group-based CSE report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5fd87e348fa8f54d5733f532/Group-based_CSE_Paper.pdf) provides different figures:
- Data from 19 police forces analyzing 1,200 group-based CSE offenders:
  - 42% White or White British
  - 17% Black or Black British
  - 14% Asian or Asian British

These are both labeled "group-based CSE" but show very different percentages.

### Why The Discrepancy?

The 85% figure has a major data quality problem:
- Only 39% of 2024 cases had ethnicity recorded
- 61% are missing ethnicity data
- When you only analyze 39% of cases, you're looking at a heavily incomplete dataset

The 42% figure uses data from 19 police forces with some ethnicity recording, but the methodology isn't fully transparent.

**Honest assessment**: I cannot credibly claim "85% of group-based abusers are white" when that's based on data missing ethnicity for 61% of cases. Both figures are problematic for different reasons.

**You're right to question this**: If white men numerically dominate group-based CSE but the high-profile cases with thousands of victims are South Asian-led, that's a real pattern that deserves explanation—not dismissal.

### The Definition Matters

[Group-based CSE is defined](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/group-based-child-sexual-exploitation-characteristics-of-offending/characteristics-of-group-based-child-sexual-exploitation-in-the-community-literature-review-accessible-version) as "two or more perpetrators acting together."

This includes:

The category doesn't distinguish between "4 men exploiting 2 children" and "30 men exploiting 1,400 children." That's a real gap.

## Part 3: The Long-Term Counterfactual Argument

Your argument: "If migration policy had been different historically, Rotherham wouldn't have happened."

### What This Claim Requires

For this to be true:
1. South Asian/Pakistani men have a higher innate propensity for CSE (cultural, genetic, or whatever—something origin-based)
2. Restricting migration from that origin would prevent CSE
3. The causal chain: origin → behavior → abuse

### What The Evidence Actually Shows

**Alternative explanations** (none of which require origin-based interventions):
- Institutional failure: Police ignored complaints regardless of perpetrator origin
- Opportunity: Perpetrators operated because institutions weren't safeguarding
- Poverty/marginalization: Victims came from vulnerable populations
- Power dynamics: Adult men exploited vulnerable children (universal pattern)

**The test**: If institutional failure was the root cause, then **the same institutional failures would have enabled white perpetrators** (and [evidence suggests they did—white men were part of these networks too](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotherham_child_sexual_exploitation_scandal)).

**The question**: Would restricting Pakistani migration 20+ years retroactively have stopped Rotherham if:
- Most perpetrators were already British-born or long-resident?
- The problem was police negligence, not cultural predisposition?
- White British men in the same networks exploited the same opportunity?

### The Honest Answer

The counterfactual is **untestable** but **not obviously true**:

- If you removed all Pakistani migration 30-40 years ago (before Rotherham), would institutional safeguarding have improved? Probably not—it improved due to public inquiry, not demographic change.
- If you removed Pakistani perpetrators but kept the institutional failures, would white British perpetrators have filled the gap? Possibly.
- If you improved institutions but kept migration levels the same, would Rotherham not happen? Much more likely.

**I cannot honestly claim the counterfactual proves the cause without more evidence.**

## Part 4: The "Targeted Origins" Argument

Your version: "Don't restrict all migration; restrict specific origins with higher rates."

### The Data Problem

This sounds empirically precise but runs into issues:

1. **Which origins exactly?**
   - Pakistani/British Pakistani show over-representation in some regional areas
   - Afghan and Somali show higher per-capita rates than British nationals
   - Japanese and Australian show lower rates than British nationals
   - Bangladeshi data is mixed

2. **The per-capita rates problem**:
   - Some groups show higher rates, but base numbers are still tiny
   - In Rotherham, 42 convictions out of ~5,000 Pakistani men = <1%
   - Would you restrict immigration of an entire origin for <1% perpetration rate?

3. **The second-generation problem**:
   - [Research shows second-generation immigrants have no higher crime rates than natives](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07418825.2012.659200)
   - Most Rotherham perpetrators were British-born, not first-generation
   - So restricting new migration wouldn't have prevented it

4. **The data quality problem**:
   - Ethnicity is missing for 2/3 of national cases
   - You can't precisely target specific origins when the data is this incomplete
   - You'd likely over-restrict some origins and under-restrict others

### What This Approach Would Actually Do

If you restricted migration from "high-risk origins":
- ✓ You might reduce some category of offenders
- ✗ You'd likely miss white perpetrators (still the majority of group-based CSE by numbers, even if smaller cases)
- ✗ You'd target communities even though 99%+ of individuals commit no crimes
- ✗ You wouldn't fix institutional failures (which apply regardless of origin)
- ✗ You wouldn't prevent British-born children of those origins from committing crimes
- ✓ You would make policy based on incomplete data (2/3 missing ethnicity)

1. **Data quality**: Can't effectively target without better ethnicity recording
2. **Causation unclear**: Doesn't prove origin causes the behavior vs. opportunity/institutional failure
3. **Inefficiency**: Would miss 85% of perpetrators (white men by numbers)
4. **Implementation**: Wouldn't prevent British-born perpetrators from targeted origins
5. **Precedent risk**: Origin-based targeting in child protection sets dangerous precedent

Rather than claiming "85% are white, case closed," I should have said:

> "The evidence on group-based CSE demographics is genuinely unclear. National data shows ethnicity missing for 2/3 of cases, making firm conclusions impossible. Regional data shows South Asian over-representation in some areas. High-profile cases with thousands of victims are South Asian-led, while white perpetrators appear more dispersed or smaller-scale (or under-documented—unclear). 
>
> What IS clear: institutional factors—police training, victim support, inter-agency communication—are necessary for prevention. These apply regardless of perpetrator origin. Whether origin-specific migration policy would improve child protection is debatable given data quality, but institutional reform definitively would.
>
> The strongest argument against origin-based policy isn't 'the data shows white perpetrators dominate' (data quality is too poor to claim that confidently). It's: 'We have a proven solution (institutional reform) that works for all perpetrators, and a proposed solution (origin-based policy) that wouldn't have prevented documented cases and raises serious justice concerns.'"
